What is SDG 15 and why are forests and meadows essential?

Sustainable Development Goal 15 (“Life on Land”) is one of the 17 goals adopted by the United Nations in 2015. Its message is simple: protect, restore and sustainably manage land ecosystems – forests, meadows, wetlands, mountains and drylands – and halt the loss of biodiversity.

Why does this matter for our survival?

Forests cover about a third of the land and are home to more than 80 % of all land-dwelling species of animals and plants. They store carbon dioxide, retain water, cool the air and protect the soil from erosion. Meadows matter just as much: pollinators live there, and without them we would have no fruit, vegetables or honey. More than a billion people depend directly on forests for their livelihood.

  • By 2030: manage all forests sustainably, halt deforestation and restore degraded forests.
  • By 2030: restore degraded land and fight the spread of invasive alien species.
  • From 2020 onwards: protect endangered species and prevent their extinction.
  • Involvement: nature conservation must become part of local plans and decisions, not just a task for the state.
🌲

Forests

Biodiversity

A variety of species is an ecosystem's safety net: the more diverse it is, the better it copes with drought, disease and extreme weather.

Sources: United Nations (Agenda 2030), IPBES – Global Assessment on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services.

Contribution by Student B

Two connected problems in our local area

As we walked through the local forests and meadows, we noticed two things that keep reinforcing each other. Both can be solved – but only if we recognise them first.

Problem 01

Forest pollution and invasive plants

In the forest we found not only discarded plastic but also small illegal dumps of construction waste, tyres and plastic bags. At the same time Japanese knotweed is spreading quickly along paths and streams.

  • Illegal dumps: waste in the forest takes decades to decompose and releases microplastics and chemicals into the soil.
  • Plastic: animals mistake it for food or get tangled in it.
  • Invasive alien plants: Japanese knotweed grows extremely fast, its roots prevent native plants from growing and reduce the diversity of the undergrowth.
Problem 02

Loss of biodiversity and pollinators

As natural habitats shrink, the number of species shrinks with them. The most visible loss is that of pollinators: bees, bumblebees and butterflies.

  • Habitat loss: construction, intensive farming and the mowing of meadows destroy food and shelter.
  • Lack of flowers: on mown monocultures pollinators find no food.
  • Consequences: less pollination means a smaller harvest, less honey and fewer species in the entire food chain.
💡

Why are the two problems connected? A polluted and altered forest loses its undergrowth, flowers and dead wood – exactly the micro-habitats in which pollinators and other insects live. When we fix one problem, we also solve the other.

Conclusion

Each of us can help keep life on land alive

Protecting forests and meadows does not require big projects – a few consistent habits and a little attention to the place where we live are enough.

  • 🚯
    Whatever you bring into the forest, bring it back out. Always take your litter with you.
  • 🌱
    Do not let invasive plants spread – report them and never carry them to new locations.
  • 🐝
    Plant native flowers on your balcony or in your garden so pollinators have food from spring to autumn.
  • 🏠
    Put up an insect hotel or a birdhouse – the shelter that nature misses in the city.
  • 🥾
    Stay on the marked trails and do not disturb animals, especially during the nesting season.
  • 📣
    Spread the word: an informed classmate, neighbour or friend is the fastest measure for nature.
